# Quality Rubric

Status: draft 0.1. The thresholds and weights are proposals. They will be tested with real reviewers before they are final.

## What this is for

Steelframe writes daily current affairs editorials for the UPSC Civil Services Examination. The first question for the project is: **is the writing good enough that an aspirant would choose it over established coaching material?**

This document turns "good enough" into a score. It is the test every editorial must pass, and the test the whole system is tuned against.

## Terms used here

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| UPSC | The Union Public Service Commission, which runs the exam |
| Prelims | The first, objective-style stage of the exam |
| Mains | The second, written stage. Answers are long-form |
| GS | General Studies, the four Mains papers that current affairs feeds into |
| PYQ | Previous year question |
| Editorial | One Steelframe piece on one issue |
| Claim | Any fact, figure, date or attribution stated in a piece |
| Reference piece | A piece from established coaching material on the same issue, used as the benchmark |

## How scoring works

1. A reviewer reads a piece without knowing whether it is Steelframe's or a reference piece (a **blind review**).
2. The reviewer scores each dimension below from 1 to 5, using the descriptions in the tables.
3. Each score is divided by 5 and multiplied by its weight, so a 5 earns the full weight. The total is out of 100.
4. At least three reviewers score each piece. The final score is the average.

## Dimensions and weights

| # | Dimension | Weight | What it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accuracy | 25 | Is every claim true, and traceable to a primary source? |
| 2 | Judgment | 20 | Does the piece weigh the points and say whether a measure will work? |
| 3 | Evidence quality | 10 | Are the examples real, specific and dated? |
| 4 | Exam fit | 10 | Does it cover the angles a Prelims or Mains answer needs? |
| 5 | Linking | 10 | Is each point tied to a syllabus topic and the past questions it answers? |
| 6 | Consistency | 10 | Does the same measure carry the same figure everywhere? |
| 7 | Readability | 10 | Is the depth right for the reader, and can it be read in the time stated? |
| 8 | India link | 5 | Is the issue tied to the Indian law, institution or case? |
| | **Total** | **100** | |

Two further checks are pass or fail for the system as a whole, not scored per piece:

| Check | Pass means |
|---|---|
| Timeliness | The piece is published the same day as the news it covers |
| Corrections | Errors are fixed visibly, with a date, and readers can report them |

## Score descriptions

Scores of 2 and 4 sit between the descriptions shown.

### 1. Accuracy (weight 25)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | One or more claims are wrong in a way that changes the meaning, or cannot be traced to any source |
| 3 | Claims are true, but some cannot be traced to a primary source, or a figure is slightly off |
| 5 | Every claim is true and linked to a primary source, and a spot check finds no error |

### 2. Judgment (weight 20)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | A list of points with equal weight. No view on what matters |
| 3 | Some points are ranked, or one counterargument is shown |
| 5 | Says which points matter most and why, shows the strongest counterargument, and states whether the measure is likely to work |

### 3. Evidence quality (weight 10)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Examples are hypothetical or vague |
| 3 | Examples are real but undated, or lack figures |
| 5 | Every example is real, dated and specific, with a figure where one exists |

### 4. Exam fit (weight 10)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reads like news. An aspirant could not build an answer from it |
| 3 | Covers the main angles but leaves out some an answer would need |
| 5 | Covers what exists, what is new, what is wrong, what to do, and a Mains angle, with Prelims facts separated |

### 5. Linking (weight 10)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | No syllabus or past-question links |
| 3 | A syllabus topic is named, but links to past questions are missing or loose |
| 5 | Each point is tied to its syllabus topic, and past questions are linked to the points that answer them |

### 6. Consistency (weight 10)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | The same measure carries different figures within the piece or across pieces |
| 3 | Figures agree, but terms or units change between sections |
| 5 | Figures, names and units are identical everywhere, including across the brief and the long version |

### 7. Readability (weight 10)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Hard to follow, or far longer than the stated reading time |
| 3 | Clear, but dense, or poorly paced |
| 5 | Clear, well paced, matches the stated reading time, and the reader can choose a shorter or longer version |

### 8. India link (weight 5)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | No link to India |
| 3 | Mentions India but not the relevant law, institution or case |
| 5 | Names the relevant Indian law, institution and case, and what it means for India |

## Pass rules

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy gate:** any piece scoring 2 or below on Accuracy fails, whatever its total | One wrong fact costs more trust than a dull paragraph |
| **Minimum total:** 70 out of 100 | A piece below this is not ready to publish |
| **Automatic publish:** a piece that meets both rules above is published without human review. A daily sample is audited afterwards | The rubric is the gate, so it has to be strict |

## The "better than" test

The goal is to beat established coaching material, not only to be good. The test:

1. Pick 20 issues. Write a Steelframe editorial and gather a reference piece for each.
2. Mix them and give them to at least three reviewers per issue, blind.
3. Steelframe passes when:
   - its average total beats the reference piece's on at least 14 of the 20 issues (70%), and
   - its average Accuracy score is not lower than the reference piece's, and
   - no Steelframe piece fails the accuracy gate.

## Worked example

Two short passages on the same question: should India widen its inflation target band? They were written to illustrate the scoring. They are not real Steelframe output, and bracketed items stand for links that a real piece would carry.

**Passage A**

> India follows flexible inflation targeting. The target is 4% with a band of 2% on either side. There are many arguments for and against widening the band. For widening, it gives flexibility. Against, it reduces credibility. Experts say supply shocks matter. For example, if onions become costly in some year, the central bank may struggle. Way forward: the government should review the framework and take all stakeholders into account.

**Passage B**

> India's framework sets a 4% consumer-price inflation target with a tolerance band of 2 percentage points either side. The Central Government notifies it, after consulting the Reserve Bank, under Section 45ZA of the RBI Act, 1934 [source: the Act]. The six-member Monetary Policy Committee sets the policy rate to meet it [source: the Act].
>
> The question is whether a fixed band suits price spikes that come from supply, such as a poor harvest, which a rate change reaches only slowly.
>
> The strongest case for keeping the band is that a clear number anchors expectations and holds the central bank to account. The strongest case for widening it is that a rate rise cannot lower the price of a crop made scarce by weather, so repeated breaches may reflect shocks, not policy failure.
>
> [The piece then states which case it finds stronger and why.] Linked past question: [GS3 question on the framework].

| Dimension | Weight | A | B | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 25 | 3 | 5 | A is true but cites nothing. B links each claim to the Act |
| Judgment | 20 | 1 | 4 | A lists "for" and "against" and stops. B names the strongest case on each side and commits to a view |
| Evidence quality | 10 | 1 | 3 | A's example is hypothetical. B's mechanism is real but has no dated case |
| Exam fit | 10 | 2 | 4 | A cannot support a Mains answer. B gives structure and a way to argue |
| Linking | 10 | 1 | 4 | A links to nothing. B links a past question |
| Consistency | 10 | 4 | 5 | Both agree with themselves. B is checkable against the Act |
| Readability | 10 | 3 | 4 | A is vague. B is short and clear |
| India link | 5 | 3 | 5 | A mentions India only. B names the Act, the committee and who notifies the target |
| **Total (out of 100)** | | **44** | **86** | |

Passage A scores 44 and fails the minimum of 70. Its Accuracy score is 3, so the accuracy gate alone would not stop it, but the total does. Passage B scores 86 and passes. The gap comes mostly from Judgment, Evidence quality and Linking, which is where the review of existing coaching material found the most room to improve.

## Why these weights

The four dimensions that most affect whether an aspirant trusts a piece (Accuracy, Judgment, Evidence quality, Consistency) carry 65 of the 100 points. Reviews of existing coaching material show that exam structure and visuals are already strong everywhere, so Exam fit and Readability carry less weight. They must be matched, not beaten.

## What is still open, and how we plan to close it

Each row has a starting proposal from desk research. The last column is what only real reviewers (serious aspirants and teachers) can settle.

| Question | Starting proposal | Needs real input |
|---|---|---|
| Do reviewers agree with each other? | Measure agreement per dimension with [Krippendorff's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha). By its usual convention, 0.800 or higher is reliable, 0.667 to 0.800 supports only tentative conclusions, and below 0.667 should be discarded. These are conventions, not laws, so set the bar before looking at results and report a confidence interval ([guide](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00977)). Use the ordinal form for 1 to 5 scores. | A pilot round: 3 or more reviewers scoring the same 10 to 20 pieces |
| How do we keep Judgment fair, since it is the most subjective? | If agreement on a dimension falls below 0.667, rewrite its score descriptions and retrain reviewers before using that dimension to decide anything. | Reviewer training sessions with worked examples |
| Do the Exam fit anchors match what examiners reward? | UPSC publishes no official marking scheme for Mains. Evaluation is anonymous and holistic. Coaching sources describe common criteria: the answer addresses what was asked, has clear structure, and ends with a way forward or balanced view ([example](https://superkalam.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-answers-for-upsc-mains)). These are coaching opinions, not UPSC rules, so treat them as hints. | Teachers who have evaluated Mains scripts, to check the anchors |
| How many claims do we check for Accuracy? | For the 20-issue benchmark, check **every** claim, since the pieces are few. In production, draw a random sample each week. With zero errors found, a sample of 59 claims gives 95% confidence that the true error rate is under 5%, and about 299 gives under 1% ([calculator](https://www.learnleansigma.com/calculator/attribute-sampling-calculator/), [paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rivest/pubs/Riv06d.pdf)). If any error is found, the sample must grow. | A decision on the acceptable error rate, and a statistician's check |
| Are the weights right? | Keep as proposed. Compare the weighted total with the reviewers' overall preference in the pilot. | Pilot data: do totals predict which piece a reviewer would rather read? |
| Are the pass marks (70 total, 14 of 20) right? | Keep as proposed. | The same pilot |
| How many reviewers? | At least three per piece. The research found no standard minimum, so this is our choice. | Pilot data on how much agreement improves with more reviewers |
| Who reviews? | At least three serious aspirants or teachers per round. | Recruiting them (an evaluation-phase task) |

The 59-claim figure comes from informal calculators and a statistics paper. For a formal audit, use an established sampling method.
